<div align='center'>
    <h1> Ratios </h1>
</div>

A ratio is a mathematical expression that **compares two quantities**, showing how much of one quantity exists relative to another. Unlike an isolated number, **a ratio describes a relationship**. It tells us how two quantities are connected rather than how large either quantity is by itself. A ratio does not represent addition, multiplication, or a completed calculation. It describes how two quantities are related.

A ratio remains valid even if one of the quantities is fixed or constant. The purpose of a ratio is to show relative size or proportion, not to require that both quantities can change. The quantities **may be able to scale, but they do not have to**. A ratio describes the relative size of two quantities. It does not require both quantities to change or be variable.

Some ratios compare two things that can both vary

- $\text{Males} : \text{Females}$
- $\text{Flour} : \text{Milk}$
- $\text{Rise} : \text{Run}$

Other ratios compare something to a fixed whole

- $\text{Part} : \text{Total}$
- $\text{Sector Angle} : 360^\circ$
- Percentages

In all cases, the ratio tells us how big one quantity is compared to the other.

The ratio

```math
3:1
```

indicates that one quantity occurs three times for every one occurrence of another.

Ratios appear throughout mathematics and everyday life. They are used to describe mixtures, populations, rates, probabilities, geometic relationships and many other forms of comparison. Understanding ratios begins with understanding that they are fundamentally about **comparing quantities**.

Ratios appear throughout mathematics and science, in each case, a ratio compares the size of one quantity with the size of another quantity. Although these concepts appear different, they all rely on comparing quantities through ratios.

| Concept        | Ratio                                |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Fraction       | Part : Whole                         |
| Percentage     | Part : Whole                         |
| Speed          | Distance : Time                      |
| Density        | Mass : Volume                        |
| Pressure       | Force : Area                         |
| Probability    | Favourable Outcomes : Total Outcomes |
| Slope          | Rise : Run                           |

Ratios are the mathematical language of comparison. Fractions, percentages, rates, slopes, scales, probabilities, and many physical quantities can all be understood as different representations of ratios. By describing how quantities relate rather than how large they are individually, ratios provide one of the fundamental tools for modelling relationships throughout mathematics.

    <h1> Recognizing Ratios </h1>
</div>

A ratio exists whenever one quantity is considered relative to another. 

Examples include,

- Apples compared to berries
- Distance compared to time
- Students compared to teachers
- A part of a circle compared to the whole circle

A useful way to identify a ratio is to ask, **"What is being compared to what?"**. If the relationship expresses "How much of one quantity exists relative to another", then a ratio is present.

## Part-to-Part and Part-to-Whole Ratios

Ratios can compare two parts of a group (part-to-part) or compare one part to the entire group (part-to-whole). These two types exist because they answer fundamentally different questions and serve different practical purposes.

Suppose there are $3$ apples and $5$ berries. The total number of fruits is $3 + 5 = 8$.


#### Part-to-Part Ratios

```math
\begin{aligned}
\text{Apples : Berries} &= 3 : 5 \\
\text{Berries : Apple} &= 5 : 3
\end{aligned}
```

This type of ratio answers questions such as,

- How does the amount of apples compare directly to the amount of berries?
- If I increase or decrease one group, how much should the other change to maintain the same relationship?

Part-to-part ratios excel at situations where you need to maintain a consistent proportion while scaling quantities up or down. The relationship between the two parts stays constant no matter how large or small the total becomes.

Common use cases include,

- Receipes, e.g. $\text{Milk} : \text{Flour} = 2 : 3$
- Mixing solutions or paint
- Population comparisons

This type of ratio is powerful precisely because both quantities can vary while keeping their relative proportion fixed.

#### Part-to-Whole Ratios

```math
\begin{aligned}
\text{Apples : Total Fruit} &= 3 : 8 \\
\text{Berries : Total Fruit} &= 5 : 8
\end{aligned}
```

This type of ratio answers questions such as,

- What fraction or share of the total is apples?
- What proportion of the entire group do berries represent?
- If I know the total, how many apples or berries are there?

A ratio only requires a comparison of relative size. It does not require both quantities to be variable. Even if the "whole" is constant (Such as $360^\circ$ in a circle), the comparison remains a valid ratio.

Useful questions they answer

- What fraction of the full circle is this sector? e.g. $90^\circ : 360^\circ$ = 1 : 4
- What percentage of the total is this part? e.g. $3 : 8 = \frac{3}{8} = 37.5\% = 37.5 : 100$
- What proportion of students passed the test? e.g. $$

Part-to-whole ratios are especially valuable for understanding shares, probabilities, percentages and fractions. Both types use the same data but focus on different relationships. Part-to-part ratios are ideal for scaling and mixing, while part-to-whole ratios are ideal for understanding proportions and shares of a total.

    <h1> The Standard Colon Form </h1>
</div>

The most common way to write a ratio is using colon notation.

```math
a : b
```

For example

```math
3 : 5
```

When you write $a : b$, by itself, it is **incomplete contextually**. To make it meaningful, you must define what $a$ and $b$ represent, $a$ and $b$ only become meaningful when tied to a context and values. So you should write,

```math
a : b = 3 : 5
```

means "3 of the first quantity for every 5 of the second quantity". The colon notation is useful because both quantities remain visible, making the comparison easy to interpret.

For example,

```math
3:5
```

can be read as,

- 3 apples to 5 berries.
- 3 for every 5.

However

```math
a : b = 3 : 5
```

should be read as,

- $a$ to $b$ equals $3$ to $5$.
- $a$ is to $b$ as $3$ is to $5$.
- $a$ and $b$ are in the ratio $3:5$.

Conclusively,

- $3:5$ is a specific ratio value.
- $a : b$ is a ratio of quantities.
- $a : b = 3 : 5$ states that the quantities have that ratio value.

A ratio can be expressed as a fraction. Every ratio $a : b$ can be written as the fraction $\frac{a}{b}$, and the value of that fraction represents the ratio. The fraction $\frac{a}{b}$ has three equivalent interpretations,

1. **Division** - $a$ divided by $b$
2. **Comparison / Ratio** - $a$ compared to $b$, or "$a$ for every $b$". More precisely, for every $1$ unit of quantity $b$, there are $\frac{a}{b}$ units of quantity $a$.
3. **Part-to-Whole** - $a$ out of $b$ equal parts.

When we have a ratio comparing $a$ to $b$, we represent this comparison to a relative amount such as,

```math
a : b = 3 : 5
```

This is interpreted as $3$ units of $a$ for every $5$ units of $b$. Such as $3$ males for every $5$ females. The ratio view of fractions is the important concept to understand here. When we compare one ratio, we compare it to the simplified ratio. 

So when we observe $\frac{a}{b} = \frac{3}{5}$, for the viewpoint of a fraction as $a$ for every $b$ can we thought of as requiring $a$ to increase every $b$ to allow for the fraction to be able to be simplified to be equal to $\frac{3}{5}$. This implies $\frac{3}{5}$ can be multiplied by some number $\frac{k}{k}$, which creates the ratio $\frac{a}{b}$.

- Therefore $\frac{a}{b} = \frac{3}{5}$
- This tell us, for every $1$ unit of $b$, there are $\frac{3}{5}$ units of $a$

So $\frac{a}{b}$ represents the amount of $a$ per $1$ unit of $b$. To illustrate this with an example,


    <h1> Ratios in Percentages </h1>
</div>

Ratios are used in percentages. Percentages, literally mean "per hundred". Therefore, a percentage is a ratio to 100. It's all division and can be used all sorts of ways. Part-to-whole ratios compare a quantity with the entire quantity of which it forms a part. Unlike part-to-part ratios, which compare separate categories such as males to females or apples to berries, part-to-whole ratios measure what proportion of the whole is represented by a particular part.

This relationship explains why fractions and percentages are fundamentally ratios. Consider a basket containing $3$ apples and $5$ berries, giving a total of $8$ pieces of fruit. The ratio of apples to total fruit is

```math
3 : 8
```

This ratio can also be written as the fraction

```math
\frac{3}{8}
```

which evaluates to

```math
0.375
```

Expressing the same proportion per hundred gives

```math
37.5\%
```

Thus,

```math
3 : 8 = \frac{3}{8} = 0.375 = 37.5\% = 37.5 : 100
```

These are not different mathematical facts, they're different representations of the same relationship. The ratio notation emphasises comparison, the fraction notation emphasises division and the percentage notation expresses the ratio relative to a standard reference quantity of one hundred.

This idea appears in geometry. A sector angle of $72^\circ$ compared with a full circle of $360^\circ$ forms the ratio,

```math
72 : 360 = 1 : 5 = 20 : 100 = 20\%
```

Although the full circle angle is always $360^\circ$, the ratio remains useful useful because it tells us that the sector occupies $20\%$ of the cirle. This proportion can then be applied to any quantity that scales directly with the angle. As the sector angle increases, both the enclosed area and the arc length increase in the same proportion. Consequently, if the sector occupies $20\%$ of the total angle, it also occupies $20\%$ of the total area and $20\%$ of the total circumfrance.

For example, suppose a circle has radius $10$ cm. It's area is,

```math
\pi r^2 = \pi(10)^2 = 100 \pi \text{ cm}^2
```

Since a $72^\circ$ sector represents

```math
\frac{72}{360} = \frac{1}{5} = 20\%
```

of the circle, the area is

```math
\frac{72}{360} \cdot 100\pi = \frac{1}{5} \cdot 100\pi = 20\pi \text{ cm}^2
```

The calculation works because the angle ratio describes the fraction of the entire circle occupied by the sector. Once this fraction is known, it can be applied directly to the circles area. This viewpoint can be viewed viewed via the colon expression,

```math
\begin{aligned}
\text{Sector Angle} &: \text{Circle Angle} &= 1 : 5 &= 72 : 360\\
\text{Sector Area} &: \text{Circle Area} &= 1 : 5 &= 20\pi : 100\pi 
\end{aligned}
```

Which therefore follows,

```math
\frac{\text{Sector Angle}}{\text{Circle Angle}} = \frac{\text{Sector Area}}{\text{Circle Area}} = \frac{1}{5} = \frac{72}{360} = \frac{20\pi}{100\pi}
```

    <h1> Proportional Ratios </h1>
</div>

A proportion occurs when two ratios are equal.

```math
\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}
```

A proportional relationship appears when,

1. A ratio is identified in one quantity
2. That quantity represents a fraction of a whole
3. Another quantity is distributed in the same way
4. The same fraction applies to both

- Ratios compare quantities.
- Proportions compare ratios.

This is interpreted as the relationship between $a$ and $b$ is identical to the relationship between $c$ and $d$. This is the formal definition of a proportion. We're not primarily talking about percentages, but equal relationships. This structure allows information from one measurement to determine another. This means two different relationships have the same underlying structure. Consider a circle with a sector of angle $72^\circ$. A full circle has $360^\circ$.

```math
\frac{\text{Sector Angle}}{\text{Full Angle}} = \frac{72}{360} = \frac{1}{5}
```

So the sector represents one-fifth of the full angle. The key insight is that a circle is uniformly distributed around its centre. This means,

- Angle determines how much of the circle is selected
- Area is distributed in the same way around the circle

So the same fraction applies to area. This is how a proportion relationship is created

```math
\frac{\text{Sector Angle}}{\text{Full Angle}} = \frac{\text{Sector Area}}{\text{Circle Area}}
```

This ratio can be used to solve for a sector area, given an angle.

    <h1> Ratios in Coordinate Geometry </h1>
</div>

One of the most important ratios in mathematics is the slope of a straight line. Slope is not merely a formula. It is a ratio describing how two quantities change together. A slope is defined as,

```math
m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}
```

This ratio compares vertical change to horizontal change. For example,

- A slope of $3$ corresponds to the ratio $3 : 1$
- A slope of $0.5$ corresponds to the ratio $1 : 2$
- A slope of $-2$ corresponds to the ratio $-2 : 1$

Each slope value is a compressed numerical form of an underlying ratio. The slope between two points is calculated using

```math
m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}
```

Given the points $(0, 0)$ and $(4, 2)$. We obtain,

```math
\begin{aligned}
m &= \frac{2 - 0}{4 - 0} \\
m &= \frac{2}{4} \\
m &= \frac{1}{2} \\
m &= 0.5
\end{aligned}
```

The slope is therefore $0.5$, this can be viewed as a ratio $1 : 2$. This means, for every $2$ units travelled horizontally, the line rises by $1$ unit vertically. Alternatively, $2 \ \text{run} : 1 \ \text{rise}$. Because this ratio remains constant, every point on the line must preserve this relationship between horizontal and vertical movement.

For example,

- Moving $2$ units right required moving $1$ unit up.
- Moving $4$ units right requires moving $2$ units up.
- ...

These relationships can be written as,

```math
\frac{1}{2} = \frac{2}{4} = ... = 0.5
```

Although the distance change, the ratio remains constant. This constant ratio is what defines a straight line with slope $0.5$. In other words, the slope is not merely a number. It is a ratio that describes how the vertical change must compare to the horizontal change at every point on the line.

These ratios for slope are very important for the interpretation of a derivative in calculus, especially for Leibniz notation. Suppose we have,

```math
\frac{dy}{dx} = 2x
```

At point $x = 2$

```math
\frac{dy}{dx} = 2(2) = 4
```
This is a ratio at $x =2$. It means that an infintesimal movement of $x$ (Represented by $dx$), results in a movement of an infinestimal movement of $y$ (Represented by $dy$), having the value of $4 \ dx$. This ratio tells us the gradient at a point. Hence,

```math
dy = 4 \ dx
```

In this example, $dx$ is not a "per 1 unit" ratio like the previous examples. Previously,

```math
\begin{aligned}
3\text{ Apples} &: 5\text{ Berries} \\
\frac{3}{5}\text{ Apples} &: 1\text{ Berry}
\end{aligned}
```

Hence, $\frac{3}{5} \text{Apples}$ occur per $1 \text{ Berry}$. This allows us to create the ratio,

```math
\frac{\text{Apple}}{\text{Berry}} = \frac{3}{5}
```

We then replace the amount of $\text{Berries}$ to solve for the amount of $\text{Apples}$ in order to keep this ratio the same. We can observe if $\text{Berries} = 5$, then $\text{Apples} = 3$. However, the calculus ratio of $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is not a "per unit" of $dx$. Instead this ratio should be interpreted as $dy = 4 \ dx$.
